1Battery Standby Calculation — Method

  1. Standby load — sum the current draw (A) of every device in its normal, non-alarm condition (panel, detectors, modules, supervisory circuit current).

  2. Alarm load — sum the current draw (A) of every device with the system in alarm, worst case (all NACs/relays that could operate simultaneously, plus continuing standby loads).

  3. Durations — standby duration is typically 24 hours; alarm duration is 5 minutes minimum for non-voice systems, 15 minutes for voice/mass-notification systems (NFPA 72 Table 4.4.1) — confirm with the AHJ.

  4. Required Ah = (Standby Current × Standby Hours) + (Alarm Current × Alarm Hours).

  5. Apply an aging/derating factor (commonly 1.2, i.e. 20%) to the required Ah to get the minimum battery capacity.

  6. Select the next standard battery size at or above the derated requirement (7, 12, 18, 26, 33, 55, 80, 100 Ah).

  7. Confirm the battery charger can recharge the battery to 80% capacity within the code-required window (typically 48 hours) while still carrying the standby load.

2NAC Voltage Drop — Method (Point-to-Point)

  1. Confirm circuit configuration: Class B (Style Y, one-way, single fed run) or Class A (Style Z, two-way loop). See Class A note below.

  2. Determine the starting cut-off voltage: UL 864 9th Edition requires secondary/battery power to supply no less than 85% of nominal voltage (24V nominal → 20.4V). The panel/power supply then has its own internal drop between that 85% value and its actual NAC terminal output (typically 0.5–2.5 VDC, panel-specific) — get this figure from the manufacturer in writing; it is rarely published. Cut-off voltage = (Nominal × 0.85) − Internal Panel Drop.

  3. List devices in the order they appear on the circuit, with the wire distance from the previous device (or from the panel for the first device).

  4. Look up round-trip wire resistance (Ω/1000 ft) for the installed conductor gauge and stranding (solid vs. stranded resistance differs) from the Wire Data tab — sourced from NEC Chapter 9, Table 8.

  5. Point-to-point (PTP) method: the current in each wire segment equals the sum of that device's current plus every device further downstream (current decreases as devices are passed) — this is what panel manufacturers use in their own calculation programs. The simpler End-of-Line (EOL) method assumes full circuit current the entire distance and is more conservative; differences of roughly 30% between the two methods are common. This tool uses PTP.

  6. Segment voltage drop = Segment Current × Segment Resistance × (Segment Distance / 1000). Cumulative voltage drop = running total back to the panel.

  7. Voltage at each device = Cut-Off Voltage (Step 2, not nominal 24V) − Cumulative Voltage Drop.

  8. Compare to the device's minimum listed operating voltage (UL listing/datasheet — commonly at least 16V, varies by manufacturer/candela). PASS if voltage at device ≥ minimum required.

  9. If any device fails: increase wire gauge, add a NAC extender/booster power supply closer to the zone, split the circuit into shorter runs, or reduce device count/candela on that run.

Class A (Style Z) note: this tool calculates the worst-case single-open-conductor fault condition, where the circuit is fed from one direction only — the standard conservative design check required by NFPA 72, since the circuit must still operate correctly under a single wire-to-wire fault. Under normal (no-fault) Class A operation the two feeds share the load and drop is lower.
